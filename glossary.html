Glossary of Autoimmune Terms

Plain-English definitions of the medical words you are most likely to meet.

Plain-English definitions

Medical language can be confusing, especially after a new diagnosis. These are short, everyday explanations of terms you may hear from doctors or read on this site.

These definitions are general and for education only. Ask your doctor or specialist to explain how a term applies to you.

A

Acute
Starting suddenly and usually lasting a short time. The opposite of chronic.
Anti-CCP
A blood test that looks for antibodies against cyclic citrullinated peptides. It is often used to help diagnose rheumatoid arthritis.
Antibody
A protein made by the immune system that recognises and attaches to a specific target, such as a germ. Antibodies normally help protect the body from infection.
Antigen
Any substance the immune system recognises as foreign, such as part of a virus or bacterium. In autoimmune disease, the immune system reacts to something that belongs to the body.
Antinuclear antibody (ANA)
An autoantibody that targets parts of the cell nucleus. An ANA blood test is often used when an autoimmune condition such as lupus is suspected, but it can also be positive in people who are healthy, so it cannot diagnose a condition on its own.
Autoantibody
An antibody that mistakenly targets the body's own tissues instead of a foreign invader.
Autoimmune disease
A condition in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own healthy cells and tissues.

B

B cell
A type of white blood cell that makes antibodies.
Biologic
A medicine made from living cells that is designed to block one specific part of the immune response, such as a particular inflammatory protein.
Biomarker
A measurable sign, usually found in blood or tissue, that can point to a condition or show how it is responding to treatment.
Biopsy
A small sample of tissue taken from the body and examined under a microscope.

C

C-reactive protein (CRP)
A protein made by the liver that rises when there is inflammation in the body. A CRP blood test shows that inflammation is present but not what is causing it.
Chronic
Lasting for a long time, usually months or years, and often needing ongoing management.
Corticosteroid
A type of anti-inflammatory medicine, such as prednisolone, that calms immune activity. Doctors usually aim to use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time because long-term use can cause side effects.

D

Demyelination
Damage to myelin, the protective coating around nerve fibres. It slows or blocks nerve signals and is a key feature of multiple sclerosis.
DMARD
Short for disease-modifying antirheumatic drug. These medicines aim to slow the progress of conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, rather than only easing symptoms.

E

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)
A blood test that measures how quickly red blood cells settle in a tube. A faster rate can suggest inflammation, but it is not specific to any one condition.

F

Fatigue
Ongoing tiredness or exhaustion that is not fixed by rest. It is one of the most common symptoms of autoimmune conditions.
Flare
A period when symptoms get worse or return after a calmer spell. Also called a flare-up.

G

Genetic predisposition
An inherited tendency that raises the chance of developing a condition. It does not mean the condition is certain.

H

HLA
Short for human leukocyte antigen. These molecules help the immune system tell the body's own cells from foreign ones. Certain HLA types are linked with certain autoimmune conditions, for example HLA-B27 with ankylosing spondylitis.

I

Immune system
The body's defence network of cells, tissues and organs that fights infection and removes damaged cells.
Immunosuppressant
A medicine that reduces the activity of the immune system. It is used to control autoimmune conditions but can make infections more likely.
Inflammation
The body's response to injury, infection or irritation, which can cause redness, warmth, swelling and pain. In autoimmune disease it can continue when it is not needed and damage healthy tissue.

M

Mucous membrane
The moist lining of areas such as the mouth, nose, eyes and digestive tract.

N

Neuropathy
Damage to or poor working of the nerves, which can cause numbness, tingling, weakness or pain.
NSAID
Short for non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, such as ibuprofen. NSAIDs ease pain and inflammation but do not change the course of the disease. They can have side effects, so ask a doctor or pharmacist whether they are suitable for you.

P

Prognosis
The likely course or outcome of a condition.

R

Remission
A period when symptoms are much reduced or have gone away. It does not always mean the condition has been cured.
Rheumatoid factor
An antibody found in the blood of many people with rheumatoid arthritis. It can also appear in other conditions and in some healthy people.

S

Specialist doctors
Autoimmune conditions are often looked after by specialists. A rheumatologist treats joint and connective tissue conditions, a neurologist treats the nervous system, a dermatologist treats the skin, a gastroenterologist treats the digestive system and an ophthalmologist treats the eyes.
Synovium
The thin lining of the joints that makes the fluid which keeps them moving smoothly. It becomes inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

T

T cell
A type of white blood cell that helps coordinate the immune response and can directly attack infected or abnormal cells.
Trigger
Something that may set off a condition or make symptoms worse, such as an infection or stress. Triggers differ from person to person and are not always identifiable.

V

Vasculitis
Inflammation of the blood vessels. It can narrow, weaken or scar the vessel walls and reduce blood flow to organs.

W

White blood cell
A cell of the immune system that helps the body fight infection and disease. B cells and T cells are both types of white blood cell.
